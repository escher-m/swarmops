Landscape

Three adjacent categories. None of them is this.

People reach for one of three things when they ask "what did the agent do?" Each is good at its job. None produces a record a compliance lead can hand an auditor.

1. LLM observability — Langfuse, LangSmith, Braintrust, Arize Phoenix

These capture traces, spans, latency and cost across agent frameworks, and several are SOC 2 Type II themselves. But they were built for prompt engineering and evaluation rather than audit retention, and being SOC 2 compliant as a vendor is a different thing from producing evidence that satisfies your SOC 2 audit. Retention is a pricing tier, not a compliance guarantee — Langfuse, for instance, tiers retention from 30 days upward. Self-hosting gives you control over storage, but that control is something you configure and operate; it does not arrive pre-hardened for audit use.

Most importantly: a trace records what happened. It does not record which policy rule permitted it, or which named human accepted responsibility.

2. AI SRE agents — HolmesGPT, Aurora, K8sGPT, Cleric, Resolve.ai, Traversal

This is the category we deliberately do not compete in — we wrap it. These tools investigate, and increasingly remediate. Approval gating is already common and often the default: no open-source tool today runs fully autonomously without human approval, and the prevailing safe pattern is for the agent to open a pull request while a human keeps the merge button. Robusta can open PRs in an opt-in operator mode; Cleric supports autonomous or approval-gated actions; Rootly requires human approval.

The gap is not that approval is missing. It is that each vendor's approval lives inside that vendor's product, in that vendor's trace format, with that vendor's retention. Run two agent platforms and you have two incompatible answers to the same audit question.

3. AI governance and GRC — Vanta, Drata, Credo AI, and newer agent-native entrants

Closest in intent, and where the genuinely new competition is emerging. Agent-native governance proxies now exist that put a policy engine in front of every tool call and land the result in a tamper-evident trail. The prevailing view in this category is that you can no longer separate enforcing a policy from auditing it: a governance layer that observes logs after the fact is already insufficient for high-risk systems under the EU AI Act, whose high-risk obligations reached full enforcement on 2 August 2026.

We agree with that framing entirely. Where we differ is scope and posture — see below.

Where we would lose

A comparison page that finds its author winning every row is a page nobody believes. Honest places not to choose us:

  • You want fewer vendors, not more. If your agent platform already gates approvals and you run exactly one and expect to keep it, its built-in trail may be enough. Our value compounds with heterogeneity.
  • You need broad GRC coverage. We record agent actions. We do not manage SOC 2 controls, vendor risk or policy attestation. We are a source of evidence for those systems, not a replacement.
  • You want to debug prompts. Use an observability tool. We record consequential actions, not every token; our record is the wrong granularity for iterating on a prompt.
  • You need it in production this quarter. We're pre-launch. Design partners, yes. Procurement, not yet.
