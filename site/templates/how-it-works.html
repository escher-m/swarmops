Architecture

How it works

Five moving parts. The agent is the one you already have.

1

The adapter intercepts tool calls

Every agent framework has one seam in common: tool invocation. The adapter subclasses the agent's own tool class and overrides invoke, capturing the exact command and the exact output the agent saw.

A subclass rather than a monkeypatch, deliberately: it appears in stack traces and breaks loudly on a library upgrade, instead of a patched attribute silently ceasing to be consulted.

2

Each call becomes evidence

Output under 8 KiB is stored inline, covered by the record's own hash. Anything larger goes to a content-addressed blob store where the SHA-256 of the bytes is the filename.

Secrets are masked before storage. A ledger that faithfully records a leaked credential has helpfully made a second copy of it.

3

Policy is evaluated, not assumed

Every action is checked against a policy document you own. The matching rule is recorded on the action, along with the SHA-256 of the policy text in force.

Default deny. Explicit denies beat matching allows, so a deny rule cannot be routed around by a broader permission elsewhere in the file. Missing facts fail closed — if we can't tell whether a change is reversible, it isn't treated as reversible.

4

Nothing executes without a recorded approval

A proposal is routed to a human. The decision, the approver's identity from your IdP, and their stated reason all become a ledger record.

The executor then re-derives its own authority: given an approve record, it walks the chain back through the request to the proposal, re-evaluates policy, and runs the command read out of the immutable evidence blob the approver actually saw.

5

The chain makes all of it checkable

Each record commits to the hash of its predecessor. Editing record N changes N's hash, which was an input to N+1's hash, and so on to the end of the ledger.

Verification runs five independent checks, because each catches a different attack: hash integrity catches edits; chain linkage catches deletion and reordering, which per-record hashing alone would miss; sequence density catches a removal by someone who could also fix the links; schema revalidation catches malformed history; and evidence re-hashing catches a swapped log excerpt in a ledger that was never touched.

Deployment

SwarmOps runs inside your network, next to the agent it records. The ledger database is yours. Evidence blobs are yours. Nothing is sent anywhere, and the dashboard makes no third-party requests at all — the fonts and scripts are served from your own host, so it works in an air-gapped environment and leaks no page-view telemetry to a CDN.

Authentication is your identity provider over OpenID Connect. The authenticated subject becomes the approver_id in the ledger — an identity your own IdP can vouch for, which is the point. The dashboard refuses to start unauthenticated unless an operator explicitly opts in, and refuses that opt-in entirely in production.
